For the first couple of weeks, standup was a source of low-grade dread. My update was thirty seconds of trying to make "I read code and was confused" sound like progress, and then I'd spend everyone else's updates rehearsing my own. I suspect every intern does this.

At some point the rehearsing stopped, and standup quietly became the most educational fifteen minutes of my day. Not because of my update. Because of everyone else's.

When an experienced engineer describes their work, they're modeling a dozen skills at once, and none of them are the ones universities grade. How to compress a day of work into three sentences that a teammate can act on. Which details matter to the team (interfaces, blockers, decisions pending) and which don't (the four dead ends you tried first). When to say "I'll take that offline" instead of dragging seven people through a two-person problem. How to say "this is going slower than I thought" without either panic or spin.

The blocker updates taught me the most. There's a real skill to communicating a blocker, and I got to watch it done well daily. A good blocker report names the thing precisely, says what was already tried, and identifies who or what can unblock it. A bad one is a vibe: "still fighting the deployment thing." The first kind gets you unblocked in the meeting, someone says "oh, that's the config flag, I'll send it to you." The second kind gets sympathy and another day of being stuck. I learned to write my blockers in the first shape before saying them out loud, and the difference in how fast problems dissolved was immediate.

There's also just the vocabulary transfer. Terms of art, names of internal systems, the shorthand a team uses for its own architecture. You can't get that from documentation, because nobody documents their own slang. Two weeks of listening at standup and the codebase's naming conventions started making sense, because I'd heard the concepts spoken about by people who'd internalized them.

My honest advice to another intern: prepare your update in one minute before the meeting, then spend the whole meeting actually listening. Write down every term you don't recognize and look it up after. Notice which updates make the room nod and which make it ask five clarifying questions, and figure out what separates them. It's the cheapest senior-engineer observation time you will ever get, and it's sitting inside a meeting most people treat as overhead.
