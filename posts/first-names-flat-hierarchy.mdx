The adjustment nobody warned me about wasn't technical. It was that on my first day, I addressed a senior engineer the way I'd address a lecturer, and the response was a puzzled look and an instruction to just use his name.

I grew up in an education culture built on honorifics. Teachers are sir and madam. Seniority is addressed, formally, every time. It trains a reflex: before you speak to someone, you compute their rank relative to yours and choose your words accordingly. That computation adds a cost to every interaction, and the cost scales with the gap. Asking a question of someone far senior isn't just asking a question. It's an event.

Then I joined a team where the founder, the senior engineers, and the intern all go by first names, and the disorientation lasted about a week. What replaced it was something I didn't expect: the flatness is functional, not stylistic.

Here's what I mean. A big part of my learning has come from plain one-on-one conversations with an engineer a few years ahead of me. Not scheduled mentoring. Just talking, the way you'd talk to a classmate, about how he approaches a problem or why the codebase does something a particular way. In an honorific culture, I would have rationed those conversations carefully, because each one would feel like withdrawing from a limited account of a senior person's patience. With the formality gone, the perceived cost of a question drops to almost nothing, so I ask ten times as many, so I learn ten times as fast. The arithmetic is that direct.

The same shows up in standups. When everyone speaks the same register, an intern saying "I'm blocked and I don't understand X" is a normal sentence, not a confession. I've watched experienced engineers say exactly that, in exactly those words, and the team just... solves it and moves on. That modeling matters. The first time I heard a senior person admit confusion in plain language, my own threshold for admitting it dropped permanently.

I want to be careful not to oversell this. Flat address doesn't mean flat authority. Decisions still have owners. Experience still carries weight, and you can tell who the room listens to. The hierarchy exists; it just isn't performed in every sentence, which frees the sentences to carry information instead.

If you're heading into your first job from a formal culture, the advice I'd give my slightly younger self: the discomfort of using a senior person's bare name fades in days, and the question-asking habit it unlocks compounds for years. Take the deal.
