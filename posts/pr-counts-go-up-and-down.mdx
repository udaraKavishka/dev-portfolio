I track my merged pull requests monthly, partly because my internship reporting asks for the number and partly because I'm the kind of person who likes a counter. One month the counter said 24. The next it said 15. Same me, same effort, same hours. The drop bothered me far more than it should have, and working out why turned into a useful lesson about metrics.

The honest explanation for the dip was boring: there were fewer doable issues that month. The backlog's composition had shifted toward large, ambiguous work that wasn't sliced into intern-sized pieces yet, and toward tasks waiting on external dependencies. My throughput didn't change. The pipeline feeding me changed. When I wrote the monthly report, I stated exactly that, one sentence, no apology. Nobody blinked. Of course nobody blinked; everyone senior already knew that PR counts measure the work available as much as the person doing it.

But I want to be honest about the two weeks before that report, because I think every junior goes through some version of it. I considered padding. There are always splittable PRs, cosmetic fixes to harvest, ways to manufacture units of merged work. The thought is seductive precisely because it works: the counter goes back up, the report looks smooth. What stopped me was noticing what I'd actually be optimizing. The count exists so the team has visibility into how my internship is going. Padding it doesn't deceive the team so much as it destroys the instrument. A gamed metric measures nothing, which means the one tool that could show my genuine progress now shows noise.

There's a version of this that stays true at every scale, as far as I can tell. Any single output number, PRs, commits, tickets, story points, is a proxy with a limited domain of validity. Inside a stable month with a healthy backlog, my PR count tracks my effort reasonably well, and the habit of shipping steadily is worth cultivating. Across months with different backlog shapes, the number mostly measures the backlog. The skill isn't refusing to count. It's knowing what the number is currently measuring, and saying so out loud before someone else has to ask.

What I settled on for my own tracking: keep the count, but annotate it. "15 PRs, month was heavy on large in-progress work" is information. "15" alone is an invitation to wrong conclusions, including my own. The annotation habit also has a nice side effect: writing one sentence of context per month forces a small retrospective I wouldn't otherwise do.

The dip, in the end, cost me nothing and taught me how I'll want to read metrics when I'm eventually the one reading someone else's.
