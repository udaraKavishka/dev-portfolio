Officially, standup ends when the last person gives their update. Unofficially, the next five to ten minutes are the most valuable recurring slot on my calendar, and they don't appear on any calendar.

It works like this: the formal round ends, and instead of everyone dispersing, someone mentions something. A new tool they tried. A model release from that week. Something they read about how another company handles deployments. A thing they broke over the weekend on a personal project. It's unstructured by design, no agenda, no slides, and it lasts exactly as long as it's interesting.

A lot of my practical education about AI tooling has come from this slot, mostly via my mentor. Not "here's a tutorial," but a running commentary across weeks: what he's using and what he stopped using, what these tools are actually good for versus what the hype says, and a framing that stuck with me, that the point is to learn *from* the tools, not just produce with them. Have the tool do the task, then read what it did and understand why. That one reframe changed how I use every assistant I touch, and it arrived in an unscheduled conversation that could easily not have happened.

I've thought about why this format works when formal knowledge sharing so often doesn't. A scheduled tech talk has a cost floor: someone must prepare, an hour gets blocked, and the material has to justify the ceremony, so marginal-but-useful knowledge never gets shared. The post-standup slot has a cost floor of zero. "I saw this thing yesterday, thirty seconds, might be useful" clears the bar. And most of what a team actually needs to circulate is exactly that: marginal, timely, thirty-second knowledge that would never survive the overhead of a formal session.

The other ingredient is that everyone's already there. The activation energy of gathering people is already paid by standup. The informal session free-rides on it. Try to schedule "informal tech chat, Tuesdays at 4" and you've killed the thing that made it work; I've read enough about failed "watercooler meetings" to suspect this is a general law. The informality isn't an accident of the format. It is the format.

For another intern, my advice is just: don't leave when standup ends. Linger. Listening is a fine mode; the sessions where I said nothing still moved things from "never heard of it" to "know it exists," and knowing something exists is most of what you need to find it later, when a problem finally matches it. If you have your own thirty-second thing, offer it. The bar is lower than you think, and clearing it a few times is how you stop being the intern who only listens.
