My internship requires a monthly timesheet, hours logged against activities, submitted with the report. I treated it as pure administration at first, the kind of thing you backfill on the last day of the month from a haze of "I guess Tuesdays were mostly coding?" Backfilling defeats the entire artifact, as I found out, because a reconstructed timesheet records your self-image instead of your time.

Once I started logging daily, in small honest increments, the sheet turned into something I didn't expect: the only objective record of where my working life actually goes. And it disagreed with my self-image quickly.

I would have told you I spent most of my time writing code. The log said the largest single block was reading: codebase inspection, documentation, tracing flows to understand tickets before touching them. I'd have guessed debugging was a minor line item. Some weeks it was a third of everything, concentrated in one or two gnarly issues whose true cost I'd have otherwise misremembered as "a day or so." The gap between felt time and logged time is enormous, and it's systematically biased. Visible output feels bigger in memory. Invisible work, reading, waiting, reproducing a bug for the fifth time, evaporates from recollection within the week.

That data changed real decisions. Seeing how much of my time went to understanding-before-changing is what pushed me to formalize it, to schedule reading time deliberately instead of treating it as guilty overhead that "real" engineers presumably don't need. Seeing debugging spikes led me to start per-issue notes, since half the cost of a long debugging session turned out to be re-establishing context I'd already built and lost. The timesheet didn't just describe my month. It kept generating the next process improvement.

There's also an underrated communication angle. When my PR count dipped one month, the timesheet held the explanation: the hours were there, redistributed into a large investigation and blocked stretches. Being able to account for a quiet month in concrete terms, rather than with a vague sense of having been busy, is the difference between a report that reads as an excuse and one that reads as information. Nobody audited me. But *I* could audit me, and that turns out to be the point.

I'll keep some version of this after the internship ends and nobody requires it anymore. Not minute-by-minute tracking, which tips into self-surveillance, but honest daily buckets. Junior engineers get told constantly to seek feedback from others. The timesheet is feedback from reality, which has the distinct advantage of never being polite.
