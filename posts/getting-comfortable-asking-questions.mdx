For my first stretch as an intern, every question I asked came with an internal invoice. Asking felt like it cost something, credibility maybe, or the asker's time, or my quota of allowable ignorance. So I'd defer questions, batch them, spend an hour trying to self-serve an answer that a teammate had cached in their head, all to avoid paying the tax.

Months later the tax is nearly gone, and the interesting part is understanding what actually changed. It wasn't courage. It was three revisions to my mental accounting, each one prompted by watching how the team actually operates.

Revision one: the price was imaginary. I'd priced questions using university logic, where asking can signal you didn't do the reading. At work, the team is delivering a system together, and a teammate stuck silently is a cost everyone pays in delay, while a question is usually a thirty-second transaction. Watching senior engineers ask each other elementary-sounding questions in public channels, cheerfully, without preamble or apology, repriced the whole market for me. If the person with ten years of experience can ask "wait, which environment does this run in?", the tax I was avoiding didn't exist.

Revision two: form determines cost more than frequency. A lazy question, one whose answer is in the README, does spend something. But a formed question, here's what I'm trying to do, here's what I tried, here's the exact error, is close to free and sometimes negative cost, because a well-formed question occasionally surfaces a genuine problem nobody knew about. Twice now a question I almost didn't ask turned out to reveal an actual bug rather than my confusion. The respondent thanked *me*. That outcome is impossible if the question stays in your head.

Revision three, the subtle one: listening is a form of asking. A lot of my unblocking happens through questions other people ask. In standups and reviews, I started tracking what the experienced people were uncertain about, and their questions taught me which uncertainties are normal at every level. Junior-me assumed seniority meant running out of questions. The observable reality is that seniors ask *more* questions per meeting than I do, just better-aimed ones. The skill that develops isn't certainty. It's aim.

There's a compounding effect hiding in all this. Every question asked early prevents a family of questions later, because answers connect into a map. The intern who asks fifty formed questions in month one has a map by month three and mostly answers their own questions after that, plus other people's. The intern who protected their image by staying quiet is still paying full price for every lookup. I've been both of these people, in that order, and the trade is not close.
